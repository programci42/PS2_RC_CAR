Phase 1 — Safe Electronics Design

The electrical side of running a PS2 Slim from a battery and turning it into a 4-motor vehicle: power architecture, fusing, wiring and the order in which to test. This document was written to stay open on the bench beside you.

What happens if this phase goes wrong: the PS2 dies permanently (wrong voltage), a cable fire starts (unfused short circuit) or the battery catches fire (punctured / over-discharged LiPo). The test order below is not advice, it is the order that must be followed.

1. Power architecture

[ 12.8V LiFePO4 battery ] | | first thing on the (+) terminal: | [ MAIN FUSE 20A ] <-- 10-15 cm from the battery | [ MAIN SWITCH ] <-- the one switch that cuts everything | +-------+-------------------------+ | | [FUSE 5A] [FUSE 15A] | | [XL4016 buck] [relay contacts] output 8.50V | | [4 DC motors] [PS2 Slim DC input] | [USB] --> relay board (data + board logic only) | [5V buck] --> GL.iNet Mango (WiFi bridge, Phase 2) GND: common, since there is one battery. Use a star topology — every GND return meets at a single point on the battery (-) terminal.

Why two separate branches?

BranchFeedsWhy separate
PS2 branchPS2 Slim + relay board logic + WiFi bridge The PS2 wants a clean, stable 8.5 V. Motor noise or a sudden voltage dip locks up the console.
Load branch4 DC motors (later fans, lamps) Motors draw 5–8× their normal current at start-up. That surge must not reach the PS2 branch.

One battery or two? One battery + two fused branches is enough. The truly critical parameter is not a separate battery but the buck regulator's input margin.

Converting 12.8 V LiFePO4 → 8.5 V you have ~4 V of margin. Even if the battery voltage dips 0.3–0.5 V when a motor starts, the buck absorbs it and the output stays fixed at 8.50 V. With a margin that wide there is no reason to spend money and weight on a second battery.

A second battery becomes mandatory only when the margin is narrow. For example, a 3S LiPo (9.9 V when empty) with a cheap LM2596 — the regulator's ~2 V dropout eats the margin and the PS2 resets the moment a motor starts. The problem is margin, not the number of batteries.

2. Fuses — glass or blade, and how to wire them

What a fuse is for (a common misunderstanding)

A fuse protects the cable, not the device. Its purpose is to stop the cable glowing and starting a fire during a short circuit. What protects the PS2 is not the fuse but a correctly set buck regulator.

Can glass fuses be used?

They can, but I do not recommend them on the motor branch. The reason is technical and matters:

Breaking capacity

When a fuse blows, the wire inside vaporises. But if the current is very high, an arc keeps jumping through the ionised gas of the vaporised metal. As long as the battery keeps feeding the arc the fuse has not really opened — the glass shatters, the arc continues, no protection happens.

Fuse typeBreaking capacityHow the arc is quenchedIn this project
5×20 glass~35 ANothing to quench it — the glass is emptyLow-current branch only
5×20 ceramic (sand-filled)~1500 ASand smothers the arc✅ Good
Blade fuse (automotive ATO/ATC)~1000 A, 32 V DCDesigned for DC✅ Best fit

For comparison: a 12 V 7 Ah battery or a 12 Ah LiFePO4 can deliver 100–250 A into a dead short. That is far above the breaking capacity of a cheap glass fuse.

Practical solution — keep the glass holder and stay safe: ceramic fuses have the same 5×20 mm size as glass fuses and fit the same holder. If you already have a glass fuse holder, keep it; fit a ceramic (sand-filled) fuse instead of glass. The only visible difference is the white/grey ceramic body.

Still, for the main fuse and the motor branch my preference is a blade fuse. Being automotive it is designed for exactly this job: 32 V DC, high breaking capacity, cheap holders, easy to find, and the colour code tells you the rating at a glance.

How to wire a fuse

CORRECT: Battery (+) ──[10-15 cm]──[FUSE]──[SWITCH]──[load] Battery (−) ───────────────────────────────── [load] WRONG: Battery (+) ──────[long cable]──────[FUSE]──[load] ^^^^^^^^^^^^ this section is UNPROTECTED — if it rubs on the chassis and shorts, the fuse is not in the circuit WRONG: Battery (−) ──[FUSE]── ← NO fuse in the negative line

Rules

  1. In the positive (+) line, in series. Never fuse the negative line.
  2. As close to the battery as possible — ideally 10–15 cm. The cable between the battery and the fuse is unprotected; the shorter that section, the smaller the risk.
  3. Order: Battery → Fuse → Switch → Load. The fuse comes first so that the switch itself and the cable to it are also protected.
  4. Each branch gets its own fuse. Main fuse + branch fuses are used together: a small fault blows only that branch, the whole system does not die.
  5. Holder connections must be tight. A loose or badly crimped terminal heats up — then it is the holder that melts, not the fuse. Use a crimping tool; do not tin the wire with solder and push it into the holder (solder cracks under vibration).
  6. Never up-rate a blowing fuse. A blowing fuse is information, not a problem. Find the cause first. Wrapping wire or foil around it is deadly.

Holder types

TypeWhere to use
In-lineCable at both ends, sits in the middle of the line. The most practical for you.
Panel mountScrews onto a box/panel, replaced from outside by twisting. Neat but not required.
Fuse boxOne hub for multiple branches. Makes life much easier with 2–3 branches.

What the letters on a glass/ceramic fuse mean

The metal cap carries a marking such as T5AL250V:

  • F = fast — for electronic circuits
  • T = time-delay — for loads with inrush current. This is the one you need.
  • M = medium

The buck regulator's input capacitors draw a short but hard surge when first connected. A fast (F) fuse mistakes this for a fault and blows on every power-up. Use the time-delay (T) type.

Blade fuses have no such distinction — by construction they are already slightly slow.

3. Current calculation and fuse ratings

Method

then:
  → round up to the next standard value
  → must NOT exceed the cable's current capacity

PS2 branch

The 7.5 A in the report is also acceptable and is a standard blade fuse value; it merely gives slightly looser protection. If your cable carries 7.5 A there is no problem.

Measure the PS2's real consumption. The 15–25 W above is an estimate. Put an ammeter (or a panel volt/ammeter) in series with the battery line and read it while role.elf is running. This single measurement pins down both the fuse rating and the run-time calculation. The 5.65 A on the adapter label is maximum capacity, not actual consumption.

Motor branch

This branch's value depends entirely on your motors and cannot be calculated without measuring. Method:

  1. Power one motor and measure its no-load current (spinning freely)
  2. Hold the shaft still by hand and measure the stall (locked-rotor) current — the worst case
  3. Normal driving current ≈ 2–3× the no-load current (load and ramps)
  4. Multiply by how many motors run at the same time
Typical small geared DC motorValue
No-load current0.1–0.3 A
Under load0.5–1.5 A
Stall current2–4 A
4 motors, under load~2–6 A
4 motors, all stalled8–16 A

With these values a 15 A blade fuse is a reasonable start: it does not blow in normal driving, and it blows in a real lock-up (jammed wheel, seized motor). Which is exactly what you want.

4. Cable selection

Motor branch~15 A2.5 mm² (~13 AWG)Must carry the start-up current
PS2 branch (battery → buck)~3 A1.0 mm² (~17 AWG)

WiFi bridge 5 V~0.3 A0.5 mm²

Do not use solid-core cable in a moving vehicle. Vibration fatigues the metal and the cable eventually breaks invisibly inside its insulation — a very hard fault to find.

Use: silicone-insulated, fine-stranded cable. Flexible, heat-resistant and vibration-tolerant. Plentiful at RC/drone suppliers.

Wiring discipline

  • Positive lines red, negative lines black. No exceptions.
  • All connections crimped; if needed add heat-shrink over them, not solder.
  • Keep cables away from wheels, gears and the PS2's fan intake.
  • Give every cable bundle strain relief — the pull must land on a clamp, not on the solder/crimp joint.
  • Use XT60 (power) and XT30 (branches) connectors. They are polarised and cannot be reversed.

5. Setting up the buck regulator

The XL4016 chosen in the report is right: 4–40 V input, adjustable output, 8 A class.

Do not trust the amp rating printed on cheap modules. A module labelled "8A" heats up and goes into protection at 3–4 A without a heatsink and airflow. You will draw ~3 A, so an 8 A class module is the right choice — but fit the heatsink and mount it where air can reach it.

Setting the output voltage — the order is critical

STEP 1

First measure the original adapter's polarity. Do not assume which contact of the PS2 DC jack is (+) — plug in the original adapter, measure the inner and outer contacts with a multimeter, write it down. You will wire the buck output accordingly.

STEP 2

Connect the buck to the battery with NOTHING on its output. The PS2 must absolutely not be connected.

STEP 3

Put the multimeter on the buck output and turn the potentiometer slowly. Most modules have a multi-turn trimpot; you may need several turns. Target: 8.50 V. Take your time, turn slowly.

STEP 4

Test with a dummy load. Connect a 12 V car bulb (or a power resistor) to the output. Does the voltage hold at 8.50 V? If it drops under load the module is inadequate. Leave it for 15 minutes and check the buck's temperature by hand.

STEP 5

Remove the load, verify the voltage again, then connect the PS2. Connect (−) first, then (+).

Do not skip this order. Turning the trimpot with the PS2 connected means a momentary 20 V reaching the console. The PS2 dies on the spot and there is no way back.

Input margin check

For the buck to work, the input must be a certain amount above the output (dropout). As your battery discharges this margin shrinks:

BatteryFullEmpty (cut-off)Margin for 8.5 VVerdict
12.8 V LiFePO4 (4S)14.6 V~10.0 V1.5–6 V✅ Comfortable
12 V lead-acid13.8 V~10.8 V2.3–5.3 V✅ Good
3S LiPo12.6 V9.9 V1.4–4.1 V⚠ Risky near empty

6. Battery choice and protection

I recommend LiFePO4 over LiPo — the 12.8 V LiFePO4 choice in the report is correct.

Reason: in a vehicle that roams, bumps and tips over, the battery will take hits. A punctured or crushed LiPo goes into thermal runaway and starts a fire. LiFePO4 chemistry is markedly safer here, is sold with a built-in BMS, has a flatter discharge curve and many times the cycle life. Think of the price difference as an insurance premium for this project.

Mandatory protection layers

  • BMS — usually built into LiFePO4 packs. Provides over-discharge, over-charge and short-circuit protection. If not built in, buy one separately.
  • Low-voltage alarm / indicator — a panel voltmeter or an audible alarm. The PS2 does not shut down gracefully when the voltage drops, it dies abruptly.
  • Main switch — rated for the sum of the branch currents (at least 25–30 A). Put it somewhere easy to reach.

Memory card warning: if power is lost while the PS2 is saving settings (mc0:SYS-CONF/ROLECFG.TXT) the memory card can be corrupted — and that card carries FreeMCBoot. When the battery alarm sounds, do not save settings; charge first.

Charging rules

  • Remove the battery from the vehicle to charge it.
  • The charger must match the chemistry — a LiFePO4 charger and a lead-acid charger are not the same.
  • Never charge unattended. On a non-flammable surface, preferably in a charging bag.

7. Motor wiring and relay contacts

Current vehicle: four RS-540 motors. The two left motors are one parallel group, the two right motors another. Only the first four relays of the Seda Elektronik 8-channel USB relay board are used to set direction. The board's 5 V power USB and data USB go to two separate USB ports of the PS2 Slim.

Relay contact = galvanic isolation

A relay contact is a mechanical switch; the coil side and the contact side are electrically separate. So the PS2 side never touches the motor power. This is the nicest part of the architecture.

The current bidirectional SPDT wiring

In this vehicle each relay selects the polarity of one motor terminal:

  • The NC terminals of the four relays are on the motor supply's (−) rail.
  • The NO terminals of the four relays are on the motor supply's (+) rail.
  • COM1 and COM2 go to the two terminals of the paralleled left motor group.
  • COM3 and COM4 go to the two terminals of the paralleled right motor group.
  • When only one of a group's two relays is energised, the terminals see opposite polarity and the motors run.
  • With both relays released, both motor terminals are (−); with both energised, both are (+). In either case the voltage across the motor is zero and it stops.

With this wiring, all four relays energising at once does not short the supply; both terminals of both motor groups are (+) and the motors stop. The software nevertheless releases all relays on conflicting commands to reach a defined, safe state.

Coil supply — check it

Verify by measurement where the Seda relay board's coils are powered from. If the coils are fed from USB: 8 relays × ~70 mA = 560 mA, above the USB port's nominal 500 mA limit. With 4 relays energised at once ~280 mA — close to the limit.

If the board has a VCC/JD-VCC jumper (most relay boards do), remove it and feed the coils from a separate 5 V line. Leave only data and board logic on USB.

The price of switching DC motors with relays

Relay contacts wear when switching DC and inductive loads (motors). On AC the arc extinguishes itself every half cycle; on DC it does not — the contact surface pits over time and eventually welds. This is an acceptable cost for Phase 1, but you need to know it:

  • Because the motor polarity is reversed, do not fit a unidirectional 1N5408 flyback diode across the motor terminals; in one of the directions it short-circuits the supply.
  • For contact arcing and motor noise, a bidirectional TVS or an RC snubber suitable for bidirectional operation can be used. Choose the voltage/energy rating from measurement.
  • Do not click the relays several times a second — the software already sends a command only on a state change, which is good.
  • The permanent solution is a PWM motor driver (with a PIC board, in a later phase).

Tank-style driving wiring

RelayRoleButtonCommand (ON/OFF)

The software interlock is active. On conflicting commands such as Cross+Triangle or Square+Circle, main.c switches the relays of both motor groups OFF. It also applies a configurable dead time on direction changes. In the current SPDT wiring, both relays of one group energising puts (+) on both motor terminals; it does not short the supply.

8. Assembly order and test protocol

Stop and verify at the end of every step. Skipping steps is the only real risk in this phase.

1

Measurements. PS2 DC jack polarity. Motors' no-load and stall current. The PS2's real consumption (with the original adapter, ammeter in series).

2

Buck setup. The 5 steps above. 15-minute dummy-load test. PS2 still not connected.

3

Run the PS2 from the battery on the bench. No vehicle, no motors, just battery → fuse → switch → buck → PS2. Does role.elf boot? Does the controller work? Leave it half an hour, check the buck's temperature.

4

Add the relay board, test without motors. Connect only a multimeter (continuity mode) or a small LED to the contacts. Do the right contacts close as you press buttons? This step catches wiring mistakes before burning a motor.

5

Connect one motor. Vehicle wheels off the ground. Are forward/reverse in the right direction? If it runs backwards, swap the motor leads.

6

Connect all four motors, test with the wheels in the air. Are left/right turns correct? Watch the current draw on the ammeter.

7

Put it on the ground, drive a short distance. Keep the first drive short, watch for heating and voltage sag.

First power-up checklist

  • Main fuse within 15 cm of the battery (+) terminal
  • Both branches have their own fuse
  • No fuse anywhere in the negative line
  • Buck output measured at 8.50 V with no load and under load
  • PS2 jack polarity verified against the original adapter
  • NC terminals on (−), NO terminals on (+); left motors across COM1–COM2, right motors across COM3–COM4
  • No unidirectional flyback diode on the motor terminals; suppression is a bidirectional TVS/RC snubber
  • Software conflicting-command interlock and direction-change dead time tested
  • All cables fine-stranded silicone, crimped, heat-shrunk
  • Cables away from wheels and the PS2 fan intake
  • Battery firmly secured, in a position that will not take hits
  • Main switch in an easily reachable spot
  • Voltmeter/alarm fitted and readable
  • Fire extinguisher or sand bucket in the same room (for the first tests)
